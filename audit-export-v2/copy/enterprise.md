Three workstreams, one problem

This wasn’t one tool. Across three internal platforms, the same problem kept surfacing: systems
that govern real decisions — staffing, remote work, hiring capacity — had grown opaque, so the
people who depended on them stopped trusting them.

Sole UX designer
RPM — Resource Planning & Management
Ubisoft’s primary staffing platform. A prior redesign changed the UI and workflows with no UX research, leaving 1,000+ users frustrated. I ran a 101-response satisfaction survey, then audited 40+ modals and rebuilt the navigation ribbon and the three core modals.

Sole UX designer → de facto product strategist
Work Arrangement — remote-work governance
The tool employees use to formally request long-term remote work under Ubisoft’s hybrid policy. What looked like a UI cleanup was a broken conversation between roles. I researched across four organizational levels and defined five structured approval flows with accountability checkpoints.

Systems & governance design
Capacity Governance — quota visibility
Quota rules that decide hiring were buried and easy to misread across studios. I defined a warning-only model — used, buffer, threshold, time scope — that informs decisions without blocking flexibility, with performance-aware patterns for large grid datasets.

What the research found

RPM was drowning in complaints, but no one had mapped the root causes. Rather than guess, I ran
a global satisfaction survey — 101 responses — and followed it with interviews.
The results overturned the obvious assumptions:

Seniority didn’t help. Long-time and frequent users were the most frustrated — time with the tool bred friction, not fluency.
Project Managers were the largest user group (38%) and among the least satisfied (3.3/5), so I ran deeper usability research with them.
Overall satisfaction sat at 3.5/5 , but the tool scored just 2.16/5 on its ability to save time — despite 62% of users being in it several times a week.
Three themes repeated: too many steps to manage positions and requests, key features people couldn’t find, and inconsistent UI behaviour that made data feel untrustworthy.

My role
Sole UX designer on RPM and Work Arrangement, working with product owners, a
dev lead, and business analysts. On Work Arrangement I became the de facto product strategist —
shaping problem framing, priorities, and the phased roadmap without a formal PO title. I owned
the research, the systemization, and the governance/workflow decisions, from messy inventory to
a coherent interaction model that the shared design-system team (EGG) could ship directly.

Design systemization
Consolidated one-off modals into consistent, reusable patterns.
Made quota visibility a first-class part of the interface, not a buried number.
Designed approval flows with clear status, ownership, and next steps.
Built performance-aware data interfaces that stayed readable under real density.

In enterprise tools, trust is a feature. If people can’t see why a decision happened, they stop
believing the system.

Workflow redesign

On Work Arrangement, the old tool supported only two outcomes — approve or reject — and
everything else (escalations, corrections, sending a request back) happened over email or not at
all. I defined five structured approval flows that make every path explicit and
every decision owned:

Did you speak with the employee? — a required accountability checkpoint before any decision is published, so real communication happens before automation.
Manager is final approver — the default, with a templated, human notification instead of a silent status flip.
Send back to employee — a revision loop that replaced “cancel and resubmit.”
Escalate to committee and escalated-then-returned — clarifying who decides versus who publishes.

Because HR policy limited what the system could show, free-text manager comments were replaced
with legally-vetted templated messaging — clarity for employees without exposing
the company.

What changed
A fragmented tool set became one coherent, trustworthy system.
Approval and quota workflows became legible to the people who depend on them.
Consistency reduced confusion and the workarounds that erode governance.

Framed in terms of the design outcomes I can speak to directly — no invented business metrics.

What I learned
A working interface can hide a broken system. RPM and Work Arrangement both “functioned” — the real failures were trust, ownership, and tone underneath. Research is what made them visible.
Constraints are design material, not obstacles. HR and legal limits didn’t kill the human solution; they reshaped it into templated messaging that was both compliant and clear.
Governance works better as a warning than a wall. Quotas that inform beat quotas that block — people route around blockers, but they act on clear signals.

The same instinct runs through my AI work: make the system tell the truth about its own state,
and trust follows.
