ABOUT

I’m interested in the part of AI that breaks after the demo.

I’m Favour Diokpo, a product designer. The impressive part of an AI product is easy to show. I care about what comes next — context, memory, files, cost, permissions, handoff, and failure — the unglamorous layer that quietly decides whether anyone keeps using it.

My thesis is simple: AI should not feel like invisible magic. It should feel like a place you can understand, enter, and direct. That belief turned into HERA — a local-first AI workspace where agents are characters in rooms, memory is readable, and every action has a receipt and a price. I designed it and I’m building it myself, which is why I can argue about backend event ledgers and sprite sheets in the same conversation.

By day I’m a Senior UX Specialist at Ubisoft, working on the internal platforms studios use to plan work, request hires, and make approvals — tools at the intersection of money, people, and game development, used by more than a thousand people. That work taught me how dense, high-stakes software earns belief, and what happens when it doesn’t: people route around it.

Work should not feel like wrestling with a machine. Good tools make people want to work. That’s the standard I hold my own product to.

HOW I THINK

Six things I actually believe.
I care about the part that breaks after the demo

Context, memory, files, cost, permissions, handoff, failure. The demo is easy. The week after the demo is the product.

AI should feel like a place

Not invisible magic — a place you can understand, enter, and direct. In HERA that’s literal: rooms, agents at desks, work you can watch.

If the system acted, show it

If the AI read a file, changed a task, saved a memory, spent money, or failed, the user should be able to see it. Anything less is lying by omission.

The game layer is a language

Rooms show where work lives. Agents show who is acting. Logs show what changed. Play is how complicated systems explain themselves.

Density can be calm

Ubisoft taught me that dense, high-stakes software can still read clearly — if the hierarchy is honest and the rules are visible.

Good tools make people want to work

Work should not feel like wrestling with a machine. If the tool feels dead, people route around it — I’ve watched it happen at enterprise scale.

I design systems that make complicated work visible.
